Lesson 3 · The learning loop

A decline tells you the wording was wrong, not that the rule is

Decision 7 is one sentence: when you ignore an offer, something should be different next time. Today nothing is, and the reason that was the right call is also the reason it now costs you.

The scene

Three turns, spread over a week. This is the whole problem.

Mon  you: shorter
     it:  Want me to add "keep architecture answers under
          200 words" under How to talk to me?
     you: (says nothing, moves on)
     ---> forgotten completely

Thu  you: shorter
     it:  Want me to add "keep architecture answers under
          200 words" under How to talk to me?
     ---> the identical sentence, with no idea it is the
          second time of asking

Thursday is the failure. Not because asking twice is rude, but because the second ask contains less information than the situation does. The brain knows something on Thursday it is not using: that this exact sentence already failed once.

Today's rule is right, and that is the trap

If they don't take it up, drop it. AGENTS.md:156

Dropping exists to prevent nagging, and nagging is the worse failure. An assistant that re-litigates every suggestion you passed on is exhausting within a day. So the manual chose amnesia over nagging, which was correct given only those two options.

Decision 7 is the claim that there is a third option, and that you get it by asking a different question about the decline.

The reframe

When someone ignores "want me to add keep architecture answers under 200 words", the obvious reading is they do not want a rule. It is usually the wrong reading. Far more often it is one of these:

  • The rule is right but the number is wrong. 200 words is arbitrary and they know it.
  • The rule is right but the scope is wrong. It is not about architecture, it is about anything where they wanted a recommendation and got options.
  • The diagnosis is wrong. The answer was not too long, it was badly ordered, and length was the symptom they had a word for.
  • They genuinely do not want a rule. This happens, and it is the least common case.

So: a decline is data about the proposal, not about the preference. It narrows the search rather than ending it. Discarding it throws away the one thing that would have made the next attempt better.

You already built this exact mechanism, for claims

A refuted assumption is never deleted. It's the most valuable row in the register: it records a specific way the model of them was wrong, and it stops the same guess being made next month. AGENTS.md:601

Read that sentence with "proposal" in place of "assumption" and it describes decision 7 exactly. The register keeps refutations because a wrong guess, once identified, is worth more than a blank. A declined proposal is the same artifact, one level down: not a wrong claim about who you are, a wrong sentence for a rule you probably do want.

This is why 7 needs no new machinery. It is the register pattern applied to proposals. If you find yourself designing a new file format for it, you have overshot.

The threshold that keeps it from becoming nagging

Memory without a bound is just nagging with better records. Two bounds do the work:

  • Nothing is recorded on the first decline. Below two occurrences of the same friction, drop it exactly as today. One ignored offer is not a signal, and a brain that files your every non-answer is the surveillance feel you are trying to avoid.
  • The same sentence is never offered twice. If the second attempt is the first attempt repeated, the memory bought you nothing and you have built the nagging you were afraid of.

What Thursday looks like instead

Thu  you: shorter
     it:  That's twice this week. Monday I offered "keep
          architecture answers under 200 words" and you
          passed on it. Is it the length, or is it that I
          lead with options instead of a recommendation?

Louder is not what changed. Better informed is what changed, and the human can hear the difference immediately. That single exchange is also the most demonstrable thing in this whole design: it is a moment no stateless assistant can produce, because it depends on remembering a non-event from three days ago.

Before you close this

Someone argues that recording declines violates "you propose, they accept" (AGENTS.md:158), because the brain is now keeping records of things the human never agreed to. Answer them.

Check yourself

The rule governs what may be written into the profile, and nothing here writes into the profile. The agreement still changes only when the human accepts a line, and a recorded decline makes that less likely to be jumped, not more, because the second offer is more accurate.

The honest half of their objection is about the raw log underneath, not about the decline record, and it is answered the same way lesson 2 answers it: by saying out loud in AGENTS.md that the record exists. A record the human knows about is memory. The identical record kept quietly is the thing they are right to object to.
